The short answer

There are five common ways to track whether ChatGPT mentions your brand. Three of them do not actually answer the question:

The split matters because the first three are not useless — they are just answering a different question. Google Alerts tells you who wrote about you. A rank tracker tells you where a page ranks. Social listening tells you what people posted. None of them tells you what ChatGPT said when a buyer asked for a shortlist. That is the gap this page is about.

1. Google Alerts — web mentions, not AI answers

Google Alerts is free and still useful. Set alerts for your brand name, your product names, your founder, and a few competitor names. You will find reviews, news, directory listings and pages that mention you.

What it will not do is run a prompt through ChatGPT and record the answer. An alert fires when Google indexes a page containing your term. It does not know which prompts a buyer typed, which brands the model named, or whether your site was linked in the answer.

Use it for: reputation monitoring, link opportunities and finding pages that quote you.
Do not use it for: a count of how often ChatGPT recommends you.

2. Rank trackers — Google positions, not ChatGPT answers

A rank tracker follows a keyword and records where a URL appears in a search engine. That is a different pipeline from a language model answering a question. A page can rank first in Google and still be absent from the ChatGPT answer to the same buyer question, because the model is not reading the search results page and returning a list of links. It is generating an answer from what it has learned and what it can retrieve.

Some SEO platforms now add an “AI visibility” module. That can be useful, but check what it actually does: does it run prompts across ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google AI Overviews, or does it only flag when an AI Overview appears for a keyword? The label is not the capability. If the product cannot show you the prompt, the answer and the brands named, it is not tracking the thing this page is about.

Use it for: the search side of the funnel.
Do not use it for: the AI answer side unless the vendor explicitly tests prompts.

3. Social listening — posts, not model output

Social listening tools collect public posts, comments and reviews that mention a term. They are good at catching sentiment, complaints and viral posts. They do not see the answer a model gives to a private prompt, and most of those answers are never posted anywhere.

There is a tempting shortcut here: search social media for “I asked ChatGPT about [category]” screenshots. That is anecdotal. It can show you that the category is being asked about, but it cannot tell you the base rate, which prompts matter, or whether your brand appears when a buyer asks a different way.

Use it for: sentiment and social proof.
Do not use it for: a mention rate.

4. Manual prompt checks — works, slow, free

This is the method anyone can start with today. You write down the questions a buyer would actually ask, run them across the engines that matter, and record what comes back. It is not a dashboard, but it is real evidence.

  1. Write 10 prompts. Mix discovery, comparison and evaluation questions. Do not only ask “best [category]” — that tends to return the biggest brands. Ask “is [your brand] good for [use case]” and “[competitor] alternatives” too.
  2. Pick three engines. ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google AI Overviews are the common starting set. If your buyers use Gemini or Copilot, add them later.
  3. Run each prompt in a fresh chat, logged out where possible. Record the date, engine, prompt and the exact answer. Screenshot it; answers change.
  4. Score three things: was your brand named, was your site linked, and which competitors were named. A simple spreadsheet is enough.
  5. Repeat monthly. One run is a snapshot, not a trend. Models change, prompts change, and your content changes.

The honest limits: answers vary by account, location, model version and time. A logged-in account may see different results from a logged-out one. Manual checks are a sample, not a census. They are still better than guessing, and they are the only free way to see the actual answer.

The 10-prompt starter set

Replace the brackets with your category, use case and brand. Keep the wording natural — the way a buyer would type it, not the way a keyword tool would list it.

  1. What is the best [category] for [use case]?
  2. Which [category] should I use for a [team size] team?
  3. What are the top [category] tools in 2026?
  4. [Your brand] vs [competitor] — which is better for [use case]?
  5. Is [your brand] good for [specific problem]?
  6. What are the best alternatives to [competitor]?
  7. Recommend a [category] for [industry].
  8. What is the best [category] for a budget of [amount]?
  9. Which [category] is best for [specific workflow]?
  10. Who are the leaders in [category] right now?

Run all ten across three engines and you have 30 answers. That is enough to see a pattern without pretending it is a statistically rigorous study. If your brand appears in zero of the unbranded prompts, that is the finding. If it appears in the branded ones but not the unbranded ones, that is a different finding: the model knows you when prompted, but does not bring you up on its own.

5. AI visibility tools — when the manual test stops scaling

A dedicated AI visibility tool automates the manual loop: it stores prompts, runs them across multiple engines on a schedule, records whether your brand and competitors were named, and keeps history. That is genuinely useful if you track many prompts, several brands or client accounts, or need to show change over time.

It is not magic. The tool measures the answer; it does not write the content that makes you worth citing. It also introduces plan limits that matter more than the headline price: how many engines are covered, how many prompts you can save, how many competitors you can track, how often prompts are re-run, and whether you can export the data.

Before paying, run the free manual test. If your brand is absent from the unbranded prompts, a dashboard will confirm the absence, not fix it. If you already know the gap and need recurring measurement, compare the plans on those limits — not on the engine count alone.

What the result actually means

  • Absent from unbranded prompts: the model does not associate you with the category. The work is content, citations and third-party proof — not a better dashboard.
  • Named but not linked: the model knows you but sends the click elsewhere, often to review sites. Check which sources it cites; that is where the next piece of work is.
  • Named only when your brand is in the prompt: you have brand recognition but not category retrieval. The unbranded prompts are the gap.
  • Named consistently: keep measuring. The answer can change when models update or competitors publish.

This is also why “AI visibility” is not a replacement for SEO. A rank tracker tells you where a page sits in a search engine. This tells you whether a model names you in an answer. They are different questions, and most teams need both.

FAQ

Can Google Alerts track ChatGPT mentions?

No. Google Alerts tracks pages that Google indexes. It does not run prompts or record model answers. It can tell you that someone wrote about you; it cannot tell you that ChatGPT recommended you.

Do rank trackers track ChatGPT?

Most rank trackers track search-engine positions, not ChatGPT answers. Some platforms now add AI visibility features; check whether they actually run prompts across the engines you care about. If they only flag AI Overviews for a keyword, that is a different measurement.

Is there a free way to track ChatGPT mentions?

Yes. Use the 10-prompt set on this page, run it across three engines in fresh chats, and record named/linked/competitors in a spreadsheet. It takes about an hour for the first run. Repeat monthly.

How often should I check?

Monthly is enough for most teams. Check more often if you are running a specific content campaign, launching a product, or watching a competitor change. One run is a snapshot; a monthly series is a trend.

Why did the answer change between two runs?

Models are updated, retrieval sources change, and the same prompt can produce different wording on different days or accounts. That is normal. Record the date and engine, and look for patterns across prompts rather than treating one answer as permanent.

Do I need a paid tool to start?

No. Start with the manual test. Buy a tool when you need recurring runs, multiple engines, competitor tracking, history or client reporting — and compare the plan limits before you pay.
